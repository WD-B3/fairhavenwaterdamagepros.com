This page covers shops, offices, restaurants, multi-unit buildings and other commercial spaces. The process is similar to a home loss, but scale, access, tenants and business continuity change how the work is planned.

What makes a commercial loss different

Commercial buildings have larger floor areas, shared walls and often multiple tenants. A leak in one unit can affect neighbors below and beside it. Business owners also have inventory, equipment and records to protect, plus customers to keep informed.

Crews plan around business hours where possible, containing wet areas so the rest of the space can keep operating when that is safe.

Local commercial exposure

U.S. Route 6 runs through town as Huttleston Avenue and carries much of Fairhaven's commercial traffic. The town's climate vulnerability assessment also anticipates fringe flooding of the commercial and industrial waterfront, such as Union Wharf.

Waterfront and roadside businesses should know their flood zone and keep inventory off the floor. If tidal flooding is a concern, the flood damage page explains contamination and safety.

Extraction, drying and protecting contents

Crews extract standing water, then dry with air movers and dehumidifiers sized to the space. Moisture maps cover walls, floors and ceilings, and affected finishes are removed only when drying cannot save them. Contents may be moved or packed out so they can be dried or cleaned.

For the drying process in detail, see structural drying and dehumidification.

Documentation for owners, landlords and tenants

Lease terms often decide who handles repairs. Photograph the damage, record moisture readings and keep a list of discarded items. These records support insurance claims and any conversation between landlord and tenant.

In multi-unit buildings, document which units were affected and where the water started. This helps if the source is in a shared line or roof.

Restaurants, retail and offices

Kitchens and bathrooms in food service concentrate plumbing and appliances, so leaks often start there. Retail spaces with carpet, drywall and merchandise have different recovery needs than offices with servers and files. Crews adjust their plan accordingly.

A grease trap or floor drain backup is a sewage issue, which the sewage backup page explains.

Safety checklist for a business water loss

Keep staff and customers out of wet areas and away from electrical equipment. ANSI/IICRC S500 treats water category and building use as part of the plan, and a business with food, records or electronics has more to protect than a bare room.

Write down what was in the affected area before anything is moved, since inventory lists support insurance claims.

  • Close the main shutoff and cut power to wet zones only if safe.
  • Move inventory, records and electronics above the waterline.
  • Post a notice and block off wet floors.
  • Photograph damage and list lost stock.
  • Call the 24/7 line and your insurer.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do you handle commercial water damage emergencies at night or on weekends in Fairhaven?

Yes. Business water losses happen overnight and on weekends, and the Fairhaven line is open 24/7. Call, describe the building and the water, and dispatch arranges the earliest response it can.

Can you work while we stay open?

Often yes, by containing the wet area. It depends on safety, size and the type of loss.

Do you document for insurance?

Yes. Photographs, moisture logs and scope notes are provided so you can submit them with your claim.

Who handles the tenant side?

Lease terms usually decide. Keep records and notify the landlord in writing.

What about inventory?

Move what is safe to move and photograph the rest. Crews can advise what can be dried or cleaned.
